## How behavioral interviews work {#overview}

Behavioral questions ask about real situations from the candidate's past, on the idea that past behavior is good evidence of future behavior. They usually start with "Tell me about a time when..." or "Give me an example of...". A good interviewer asks one question, lets the candidate finish, then probes with follow-ups such as "What did you do next?", "What was the result?" or "What would you do differently?". Hypothetical questions ("What would you do if...") are a different style and are easier to answer with generic statements.

## Structuring answers with STAR {#star-method}
tags: behavioral

STAR is a common way to structure an answer: Situation (brief context), Task (the candidate's responsibility or goal), Action (the specific steps the candidate personally took) and Result (the outcome, ideally measured, plus what was learned). Most of the answer should be about the Action. A strong answer describes one specific situation, uses "I" for the candidate's own contribution, gives a concrete result such as a number, date or quality change, and is honest about mistakes.

## Common behavioral themes {#common-themes}
tags: behavioral

Typical themes include working through a disagreement with a teammate, handling a missed deadline or a failure, dealing with ambiguity or incomplete information, receiving critical feedback, going beyond the assigned scope, prioritizing competing tasks, and learning a new skill quickly. Interviewers often ask for a second example when the first one is vague or the candidate's own role in it is unclear.
